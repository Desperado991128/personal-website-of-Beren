Daily Concept: Fixation detection algorithms

1. What is it?
Fixation detection algorithms are computational methods used to classify raw eye-tracking data into distinct temporal phases of human gaze behavior. They identify periods when the eye remains relatively still on a specific location, known as a fixation, separating them from rapid movements called saccades. In gaze and language model research, these algorithms transform continuous gaze coordinate streams into discrete tokens of human visual attention.

2. How does it work?
These algorithms typically operate on a time-series of gaze coordinates \((x_t, y_t)\) sampled at high frequencies. A common approach is the velocity-based algorithm, which calculates the instantaneous velocity between consecutive points using \(v_t = \sqrt{(x_t - x_{t-1})^2 + (y_t - y_{t-1})^2}\). If the velocity \(v_t\) falls below a predefined threshold \(\theta\), the corresponding samples are classified as part of a fixation. More advanced ML approaches, such as Hidden Markov Models or neural networks, learn these thresholds dynamically to handle noise and individual differences in eye anatomy.

3. Why does it matter?
In the intersection of gaze and large language models, fixations serve as a proxy for cognitive processing and reading effort. By extracting fixations on specific words or UI elements, researchers can align human reading patterns with LLM tokenization to evaluate model interpretability or train human-aligned attention mechanisms. This allows AI systems to leverage human gaze data for tasks like readability assessment or cognitive state prediction.

4. Key insight
Fixation detection bridges the gap between continuous human visual perception and discrete computational language models. It effectively translates raw eye movements into attention tokens that LLMs can process and learn from.

Paper Summaries

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we directly predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at rather than relying on intermediate gaze zone or point-of-gaze estimations? The paper aims to solve the problem of semantically mapping driver visual attention to real-world traffic objects in real driving scenarios.

2. Positioning
Existing driver gaze estimation studies typically represent gaze as a geometric vector, point-of-gaze, or a predefined gaze zone. This work fills the gap by shifting from geometric gaze estimation to semantic object-level gaze prediction, providing a more meaningful representation of situational awareness in complex traffic scenes.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is TransGaze-Object, an end-to-end Transformer-based framework that directly predicts attended traffic objects from driver face and scene data. Additionally, the authors introduce a new benchmark dataset called Urban Driving-Face Scene Gaze (UD-FSG), which provides synchronized driver-face and traffic-scene images paired with 2D gaze coordinates and gaze object labels.

4. Methodology
The framework extracts facial features, specifically face and iris-weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. It then uses a Transformer-based cross-attention mechanism to compute similarity scores and attention weights between the driver features and the traffic objects to predict the final gaze object.

5. Limitations
The proposed model achieves an overall accuracy of \(60\%\), indicating there is still significant room for improvement in complex real-world driving environments. The reliance on a single new dataset may also limit generalization to different geographic driving conditions or vehicle cabin layouts. Additionally, the model may still struggle with accurately distinguishing between closely spaced or overlapping traffic objects.

6. Critical Evaluation
The strength of this work lies in its elegant shift from traditional geometric gaze estimation to direct semantic object prediction using Transformer cross-attention, yielding a notable \(49.7\%\) relative reduction in background confusion errors. However, the relatively low absolute accuracy of \(60\%\) suggests that the facial and eye feature extraction might not be robust enough against varied lighting, head poses, or occlusions typical in real driving.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does adapting language models specifically for Cantonese improve their ability to predict human reading patterns compared to more general or Mandarin-oriented models?

2. Positioning
The paper builds on the use of autoregressive language models to model human reading expectations via information-theoretic measures. It addresses the specific gap in Cantonese psycholinguistic modeling where previous NLP evaluations showed mixed results regarding the benefits of Cantonese-specific training over general models.

3. Key Contribution
The study provides a naturalistic eye-tracking evaluation of Cantonese-adapted LLMs. It demonstrates that more extensive Cantonese-specific training correlates with better predictive fit for human reading behavior while highlighting that model rankings vary depending on the specific information-theoretic metric used.

4. Methodology
The researchers compare two pairs of models, a lightly adapted pair (CKIP GPT-2 Tiny and JED351) and an extensively adapted pair (Qwen2.5-7B and CantoneseLLM-7B), using naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data. They extract four metrics from these models, specifically lexical surprisal, POS surprisal, entropy before the target, and entropy reduction, to evaluate their alignment with human gaze data.

5. Limitations
The study is limited to evaluating only two pairs of models, which may not represent the full landscape of available Cantonese or general LLMs. Additionally, the reliance on specific information-theoretic metrics means the results are highly dependent on the chosen measures and the quality of the naturalistic eye-tracking dataset.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is a valuable intersection of gaze-tracking and LLM evaluation, providing empirical evidence that language-variety-specific training enhances psycholinguistic alignment. However, the conflicting results across different metrics suggest that the relationship between LLM training and human cognitive processing is complex and not fully captured by any single metric.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How do gaze behaviors and landmark preferences differ between individuals with good and poor sense of direction (SOD). How can mixed-reality systems leverage these differences to improve spatial learning for poor-SOD users?

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over the development of cognitive maps. This work bridges spatial cognition research and mixed-reality interfaces by focusing on spatial knowledge acquisition rather than just wayfinding efficiency. It specifically targets the navigational struggles of users with a poor sense of direction.

3. Key Contribution
The paper introduces LandmarkLens. It is a mixed-reality navigation system that uses a vision-language model (VLM) to identify and highlight navigation-relevant landmarks based on empirical gaze behavior data. The main novelty lies in using eye-tracking insights from different SOD groups to inform a VLM that actively guides user attention toward effective spatial cues.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a virtual reality study with 20 participants to track eye movements and verbal landmark identifications across four Tokyo neighborhoods. They used these gaze and speech insights to inform the design of LandmarkLens. The system then employs a vision-language model to dynamically detect and highlight effective landmarks in a mixed-reality view for a follow-up evaluation with eight poor-SOD participants.

5. Limitations
The evaluation study is limited by a small sample size of eight poor-SOD participants. The research also focuses specifically on landmark-level spatial knowledge. This represents only a first step toward broader spatial learning and full cognitive map building.

6. Critical Evaluation
The integration of eye-tracking data with a vision-language model for dynamic landmark highlighting is a highly innovative approach to spatial learning. A major strength is the user-centered design driven by empirical gaze differences between ability groups. However, the reliance on a very small follow-up sample size leaves questions regarding the generalizability and long-term retention of the spatial learning improvements.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper aims to determine if a radiologist's level of expertise can be objectively predicted from their 3D gaze patterns while interpreting thoracic CT scans. The core question is whether integrating visual search behavior into a transformer model can accurately classify radiologist skill levels.

2. Positioning
Prior eye-tracking research in medical imaging has primarily focused on 2D images, leaving the assessment of expertise in volumetric 3D CT interpretation largely unexplored. This work fills the gap by extending gaze-informed AI from 2D to 3D volumetric contexts. It connects visual search behavior analysis with modern transformer architectures to enable process-based skill assessment.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D CT reading sessions. It introduces two new techniques for integrating eye-tracking data into the DINOv2 vision backbone. These are a learnable log-space bias in the self-attention mechanism and a gaze-weighted pooling method for patch embeddings.

4. Methodology
The authors utilize a DINOv2 backbone to process volumetric CT data. Radiologist fixation patterns are incorporated into the model via a learnable log-space bias added to the self-attention layer. Additionally, the model aggregates patch embeddings using gaze-weighted pooling. The framework was trained and evaluated on 182 CT reading sessions from five radiologists of varying experience.

5. Limitations
The study is limited by a relatively small sample size of only five radiologists and 182 reading sessions. This could restrict the generalizability of the model to broader populations or different clinical settings. The approach also relies on high quality eye-tracking hardware and calibration, which may not be readily available in standard clinical environments.

6. Critical Evaluation
The work is highly innovative in its technical integration of 3D gaze data into a self-supervised transformer. Achieving an ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and an F1 score of \(0.86\) demonstrates strong predictive power for expertise classification. However, the practical deployment of such a system faces challenges regarding eye-tracking hardware constraints and the need for larger scale validation across diverse radiology departments.

Matched Outcomes, Divergent Gaze: How Foveated MLLMs Search Compared to Humans

Authors: Mohamed Amine Kerkouri, Marouane Tliba, Aladine Chetouani et al.
arXiv: 2608.16514v1

Summary

### 1. Research Question. The paper investigates whether Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) perform visual search similarly to humans when given identical foveated visual input. Specifically, it asks if models can match human search outcomes while also replicating the temporal and spatial dynamics of human gaze.

### 2. Positioning. Existing research often evaluates MLLMs using answer-alignment or saliency metrics, which focus on spatial outcomes rather than the temporal process of looking. This work fills a gap by directly comparing the scanpaths of MLLMs to human eye movements under strictly matched foveated viewing conditions. It challenges the assumption that matching visual acuity or spatial attention is sufficient to certify a model as a valid representation of human vision.

### 3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is the empirical demonstration that MLLMs and humans dissociate along a process axis, achieving equal or better target detection while exhibiting fundamentally different gaze dynamics. The authors show that MLLMs produce low-entropy, large-amplitude scanpaths that are highly self-consistent, revealing a single-pass, non-serial architecture rather than a human-like serial search process. This finding proves that standard zero-shot models are unsuitable for answering temporal, process-level questions about human vision.

### 4. Methodology. The researchers evaluated three general-purpose MLLMs on the COCO-Search18 dataset for goal-directed visual search. They drove each model fixation by fixation through a human-matched foveated view, ensuring the model received the same degraded peripheral input as a human retina. Performance was assessed across three axes including target presence decisions, target acquisition efficiency, and the gaze process itself.

### 5. Limitations. The study is limited by its reliance on only three general-purpose MLLMs, which may not represent the full landscape of architectures designed specifically for visual processing. Additionally, the authors note that no degradation regime successfully recovers human-like search at human-like success rates, suggesting the fundamental architectural mismatch is difficult to bypass. The evaluation is also constrained to the COCO-Search18 dataset, leaving generalizability to other visual tasks uncertain.

### 6. Critical Evaluation. A major strength of this work is its rigorous experimental design, specifically the fixation-by-fixation driving of MLLMs through foveated inputs to isolate architectural differences from acuity limits. The distinction between spatial and temporal evaluation metrics is a crucial insight for the gaze-tracking and LLM communities. However, a weakness is that it primarily diagnoses the problem without proposing a concrete architectural solution to make MLLMs process visual information serially like humans.
