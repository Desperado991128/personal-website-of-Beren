Daily Concept: Eye tracking in multimodal AI

1. What is it?
Eye tracking in multimodal AI refers to the integration of human gaze data as an explicit input modality within multimodal large language models. It captures where a person is looking on a screen or in a scene and feeds this spatial and temporal data into the model alongside text and image inputs. This allows the AI to align human visual attention with linguistic reasoning to improve tasks like visual question answering or image captioning.

2. How does it work?
The mechanism starts by sampling raw gaze coordinates over time and mapping them onto a 2D spatial grid aligned with the visual input. These coordinates are converted into a sequence of discrete fixation tokens or a continuous spatial heatmap. The model projects these gaze representations into the same embedding dimension as the visual and text tokens using a linear transformation such as \( \mathbf{E}_{gaze} = \mathbf{W}_{g} \mathbf{X}_{gaze} + \mathbf{b}_{g} \). During inference, the multimodal LLM concatenates these embeddings and applies self-attention, allowing the language model to condition its output generation on human visual saliency.

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating gaze into multimodal LLMs bridges the gap between passive object recognition and active human-like visual understanding. It enables AI systems to prioritize relevant visual features, reducing computational load and improving accuracy in complex visual reasoning tasks. Furthermore, this integration opens pathways for personalized AI that can adapt to individual user intent and cognitive load based on real-time eye movement patterns.

4. Key insight
Eye tracking transforms multimodal AI from a passive observer into an active participant in human visual cognition. By embedding human attention directly into the latent space of LLMs, we teach the model not just what is in an image, but how humans choose to look at it.

Paper Summaries

Learning to Refer from Estimated Listener Gaze

Authors: Téa Wright, Alane Suhr
arXiv: 2609.14207v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Can implicit signals of listener comprehension, specifically eye gaze scanpaths, be used to train vision-language models to generate more efficient and pragmatically optimal referring expressions?

2. Positioning
Existing language generation models typically rely on explicit success signals to learn how to communicate effectively. This work fills a gap by utilizing the implicit, real-time process of human comprehension as a training signal. It bridges the gap between eye-tracking research and pragmatic language generation in multimodal AI.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel reinforcement learning framework that uses an estimated human gaze model to fine-tune vision-language models. By transforming gaze fixations into token- and sequence-level rewards, the model learns to produce highly efficient referring expressions. The authors demonstrate a reduction in sequence length from \(15.4\) to \(4.0\) words while increasing referential success from \(75.2\%\) to \(80.0\%\).

4. Methodology
The authors train a speaker policy by sampling referring expressions conditioned on images and target objects. A neural listener model estimates human gaze behavior by mapping the image and sampled text to a scanpath represented as a sequence of fixations aligned to each word. These fixation sequences are then converted into reward signals to optimize the speaker policy parameters.

5. Limitations
The abstract does not explicitly state limitations, but a potential issue is the dependency on an accurate neural listener model to estimate human gaze. If the gaze estimator is biased or flawed, the speaker policy will learn suboptimal pragmatic behaviors. Additionally, the approach requires aligned multimodal data and may face scalability challenges in more complex conversational settings.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is highly innovative in its use of implicit cognitive signals like eye tracking to improve the pragmatics of LLMs. A major strength is the dramatic improvement in reference efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. However, the reliance on simulated gaze rather than real-time human feedback during training might limit the authenticity of the learned pragmatic strategies.

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper investigates whether driver gaze can be directly predicted at the object level from facial and traffic scene features. It questions the traditional approach of estimating an intermediate Point-of-Gaze and subsequently associating it with traffic objects.

2. Positioning
Existing driver gaze estimation studies typically represent gaze as a generic gaze zone or a Point-of-Gaze. This work argues that object-level gaze provides a more semantically meaningful representation of visual attention. It fills the gap of directly linking driver visual attention to specific traffic entities like vehicles or pedestrians in real driving scenarios.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the TransGaze-Object framework. It is an end-to-end Transformer based model that directly predicts the attended traffic object. Additionally, the authors introduce a new benchmark dataset called Urban Driving-Face Scene Gaze (UD-FSG) containing synchronized driver face and traffic scene images with object level gaze labels.

4. Methodology
The proposed framework extracts facial features such as face and iris weighted eye features alongside spatial features of traffic objects. A Transformer based cross-attention mechanism computes similarity scores and attention weights between these features. This allows the model to directly output the predicted gaze object without calculating an intermediate 2D gaze coordinate.

5. Limitations
The abstract reports an overall accuracy of \(60\%\), meaning the model mispredicts the gaze object \(40\%\) of the time. While it reduces background confusion, predicting fine-grained gaze targets in complex urban environments remains challenging. The reliance on a single newly proposed dataset also raises questions about generalization to different geographic locations or lighting conditions.

6. Critical Evaluation
The transition from estimating a Point-of-Gaze to directly predicting semantic gaze objects is a highly practical advancement for driver assistance systems. The use of Transformer cross-attention to fuse driver eye features with scene object features is a strong architectural choice. However, the \(60\%\) accuracy indicates that robust real world deployment is still a distant goal and further refinement is needed to handle complex visual distractions.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does language-variety-specific training in autoregressive language models improve their ability to predict human reading behavior compared to general-purpose or Mandarin-oriented models?

2. Positioning
Information-theoretic measures from LLMs are commonly used to model human reading expectations, but the benefit of adapting models to specific language varieties remains unclear. For Cantonese, previous NLP evaluations have shown mixed results regarding the advantages of Cantonese-specific training over Mandarin or general models. This work fills this gap by directly evaluating these models against naturalistic human eye-tracking data.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is an empirical demonstration that the extent of Cantonese-specific training in LLMs correlates with better prediction of human reading patterns, though this depends on the metric used. By comparing two pairs of models with varying degrees of Cantonese adaptation, the authors show that CantoneseLLM-7B provides the best fit for lexical surprisal and a joint four-metric model. Meanwhile, a smaller model performs better for entropy reduction.

4. Methodology
The authors use naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data to evaluate two pairs of models representing different levels of Cantonese adaptation. From each model, they extract four information-theoretic measures. These include lexical surprisal (\(S_{lex}\)), part-of-speech surprisal (\(S_{POS}\)), entropy before the target (\(H_{pre}\)), and entropy reduction (\(\Delta H\)). They then compare how well these metrics predict human gaze behavior across the different models.

5. Limitations
The study is limited by its focus on only two pairs of models, which may not capture the full landscape of available Cantonese-adapted LLMs. Additionally, the results are highly metric-dependent, meaning that the apparent benefit of extensive training is not universal across all information-theoretic measures. The reliance on specific eye-tracking datasets also raises questions about generalizability to other reading contexts or languages.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is a valuable addition to psycholinguistic NLP, bridging the gap between LLM adaptation and human gaze prediction in low-resource languages like Cantonese. A major strength is the systematic comparison of model pairs with varying adaptation scales, providing nuanced insights into how training depth affects psycholinguistic alignment. However, a weakness is the conflicting results across metrics, which complicates the conclusion that more training is strictly better for predicting reading behavior.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we assist individuals with a poor sense of direction in building cognitive maps for spatial navigation? The core research question asks whether guiding visual attention to navigation relevant landmarks can improve spatial knowledge acquisition for this population.

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over spatial learning, which leaves users dependent rather than empowered. This work fills the gap by linking gaze behavior analysis with advanced vision language models to create a system that actively supports spatial learning. It shifts the focus from simply getting to a destination to helping users build lasting cognitive maps.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is LandmarkLens, a mixed reality navigation system that uses a vision language model to identify and highlight navigation relevant landmarks in real time. The authors also provide empirical findings on how gaze behavior and landmark preferences systematically differ between individuals with good and poor senses of direction. This demonstrates that guided landmark attention can support spatial knowledge acquisition.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a virtual reality study with 20 participants, tracking their gaze behavior and verbal landmark identifications across four Tokyo neighborhoods. Using these gaze and behavioral insights, they developed LandmarkLens by employing a vision language model to detect and highlight useful landmarks in a mixed reality interface. They then evaluated the system with eight poor sense of direction participants to measure improvements in scene recognition.

5. Limitations
The follow up study only included eight participants with a poor sense of direction, which limits the statistical power of the findings. Additionally, scene recognition is used as a proxy for spatial learning, representing only a first step toward broader cognitive map building. The reliance on vision language models in dynamic mixed reality environments also introduces potential latency and recognition accuracy challenges.

6. Critical Evaluation
A major strength of this work is the principled integration of eye tracking data with vision language model capabilities to address a real human factors problem. The transition from observational gaze studies to a functional mixed reality system is highly compelling. However, the small evaluation sample size and the narrow definition of spatial learning limit the generalizability of the results.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Can we predict a radiologist's level of expertise by analyzing their 3D gaze patterns while interpreting volumetric CT scans? The paper aims to solve the problem of objective, process-based expertise assessment in radiology.

2. Positioning
While eye-tracking is widely studied in 2D medical imaging, its application to volumetric 3D CT interpretation remains limited. This work bridges the gap between traditional 2D gaze analysis and modern 3D volumetric feature learning. It fills a gap by introducing a process-based evaluation method rather than relying solely on final diagnostic outcomes.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D fixation data. The novelty lies in integrating gaze data into a DINOv2 backbone through two specific mechanisms. These mechanisms are a learnable log-space bias in self-attention and gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings.

4. Methodology
The approach uses a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone to process volumetric CT data. Radiologist gaze points are incorporated into the model via a learnable log-space bias that modifies the self-attention mechanism and gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings. The system was trained and evaluated on 182 CT reading sessions from five radiologists with varying experience levels.

5. Limitations
The abstract does not explicitly list limitations, but potential constraints include the small sample size of only five radiologists and 182 reading sessions. Generalization to other medical imaging modalities or different clinical settings might be restricted. Additionally, the reliance on specialized eye-tracking hardware could limit real-world deployment outside of research environments.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is a strong intersection of eye-tracking and transformer architectures, achieving impressive metrics with an ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and an F1 score of \(0.86\). A key strength is the elegant integration of gaze data directly into the attention mechanism rather than treating it as a separate post-hoc feature. However, the small participant pool and reliance on specific hardware are weaknesses that future research should address.
