Daily Concept: Eye tracking in multimodal AI

1. What is it?
Eye tracking in multimodal AI involves integrating human gaze data as an explicit input modality within large language models. It allows an AI system to process where a human is looking on a screen or in an environment alongside traditional text and image inputs. This creates a shared understanding of visual attention between the user and the AI.

2. How does it work?
Gaze data, typically captured as 2D coordinates or heatmaps, is mapped to the spatial dimensions of the visual input. This gaze map is encoded as a spatial prior that modulates the visual token embeddings generated by the vision encoder. Mathematically, a gaze weight matrix \( G \) is element-wise multiplied with the visual features \( V \) to produce gaze-aware embeddings \( V' = V \odot G \). These gaze-weighted visual tokens are then concatenated with text tokens and processed by the LLM transformer architecture.

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating gaze into LLMs bridges the gap between human visual intent and machine reasoning. It enables models to resolve ambiguous references in conversation, such as understanding what a user means by a word like "this" when they look at a specific object. This leads to more intuitive human-computer interaction and significantly improves performance in visually grounded dialogue tasks.

4. Key insight
Gaze data acts as a natural attention mechanism that directly aligns human visual intent with the internal token processing of large language models. It transforms passive visual inputs into an active, user-centric grounding signal.

Paper Summaries

Learning to Refer from Estimated Listener Gaze

Authors: Téa Wright, Alane Suhr
arXiv: 2609.14207v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Can a vision-language model learn to generate more concise and effective referring expressions by using a listener's estimated eye gaze as an implicit learning signal? The paper investigates how to transform incremental gaze observations into rewards for training better speaker models.

2. Positioning
This work sits at the intersection of gaze-based cognitive modeling and vision-language model finetuning. While previous research typically trains speaker models using explicit signals of communicative success or failure, this paper fills a gap by utilizing the implicit and real-time process of human comprehension. It leverages neural gaze estimation to create a feedback loop for language generation without requiring constant human-in-the-loop evaluation.

3. Key Contribution
The primary contribution is a novel reinforcement learning framework that converts estimated listener gaze scanpaths into token-level and sequence-level rewards for optimizing a speaker policy. By training a neural listener to predict human fixations for each word in a referring expression, the authors successfully use this gaze model to finetune a vision-language model. This results in significantly more efficient communication, reducing expression length from 15.4 to 4.0 words while improving referential success from 75.2% to 80.0%.

4. Methodology
The authors finetune a vision-language model by sampling referring expressions conditioned on an image and a target object. A neural listener model then estimates human gaze behavior by predicting a sequence of fixations corresponding to each word in the sampled expression. These fixation sequences are converted into mathematical rewards, such as \(R_{token}\) and \(R_{seq}\), which are used to update the speaker policy parameters.

5. Limitations
The framework heavily depends on the accuracy and fidelity of the neural gaze-estimating listener model, meaning any biases in gaze prediction will directly impact the speaker policy. The approach is also currently validated only on referring expression generation tasks, leaving its applicability to broader conversational or open-ended dialogue scenarios untested. Additionally, the reliance on simulated gaze might miss complex cognitive nuances of real human comprehension.

6. Critical Evaluation
This research offers a compelling bridge between cognitive science and large language model training, demonstrating that implicit human signals like gaze can effectively guide language generation. The quantitative improvements are impressive, particularly the drastic reduction in sequence length while maintaining or improving accuracy. However, the method's reliance on a learned gaze surrogate means the speaker model is only as good as the listener model's gaze predictions, potentially limiting its generalizability to unseen visual contexts.

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we directly predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at, rather than just estimating a point-of-gaze coordinate? The paper aims to solve the problem of accurately determining driver visual attention at a semantic object level in real driving scenarios.

2. Positioning
Previous studies estimate driver gaze using gaze zones or point-of-gaze coordinates. This work fills the gap by moving beyond intermediate coordinates to provide semantically meaningful, object-level gaze representations. It argues that directly predicting the attended object, such as a pedestrian or traffic signal, is more effective than mapping a 2D point to an object post hoc.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is an end-to-end Transformer based framework called TransGaze-Object that directly predicts driver gaze objects. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset named UD-FSG, which provides synchronized face and scene data with object bounding boxes and gaze labels. The framework demonstrates that bypassing intermediate point-of-gaze estimation yields higher accuracy and lower background confusion.

4. Methodology
The framework extracts facial features, specifically face and iris weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. It then uses a Transformer based cross attention mechanism to compute similarity scores and attention weights between the driver features and scene objects. This mechanism allows the model to directly output the specific traffic object the driver is attending to.

5. Limitations
The paper reports an overall accuracy of \(60\%\), indicating that nearly half of the predictions are still incorrect, which may limit deployment in safety critical systems. While the error rate for background confusion is reduced, the model might still struggle with dense traffic scenes where objects heavily overlap. The framework also relies on the availability of synchronized, high quality face and scene camera data, which may not always be feasible in lower tier vehicles.

6. Critical Evaluation
The transition from geometric point estimation to semantic object prediction using Transformer cross attention is a strong conceptual advance for driver monitoring systems. By jointly analyzing facial cues and scene context, the model effectively mitigates the background confusion that plagues traditional point-of-gaze association methods. However, the modest \(60\%\) accuracy suggests that the model may need further refinement, such as integrating temporal tracking or larger pre trained vision language features, before it can be considered robust for real world autonomous driving applications.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question (1-2 sentences)
Does adapting language models specifically for Cantonese improve their ability to predict human reading behavior compared to general Mandarin-oriented or general-purpose models?

2. Positioning (2-3 sentences)
Information-theoretic measures from autoregressive language models are widely used to model human reading expectations. However, it remains unclear if language-variety-specific training improves psycholinguistic alignment for Cantonese. This work fills this gap by directly comparing base and Cantonese-adapted models using naturalistic eye-tracking data.

3. Key Contribution (2-3 sentences)
The paper provides a rigorous eye-tracking evaluation of within-family model pairs to isolate the effect of Cantonese-specific adaptation. It shows that more extensive Cantonese pretraining and instruction tuning generally improves predictive fit for reading behavior. The findings also highlight that model rankings fluctuate depending on the specific information-theoretic metric evaluated.

4. Methodology (2-3 sentences)
The authors compare two pairs of models using naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data. The pairs are a lightly adapted model versus its base and an extensively adapted model versus its base. From each model they derive four metrics to predict reading behavior. These metrics are lexical surprisal \(S(w)\), POS surprisal, entropy before the target \(H_{pre}\), and entropy reduction \(\Delta H\).

5. Limitations (2-3 sentences)
The study reveals that model rankings depend heavily on the specific information-theoretic measure being evaluated. For instance while lexical surprisal favors extensively adapted models entropy reduction actually favors a smaller lightly adapted model. The evaluation is also limited to specific within-family model pairs which may not generalize to other architectures.

6. Critical Evaluation (2-3 sentences)
This work is a valuable addition to psycholinguistic LLM research by offering a controlled cross-model evaluation using gaze data. Its primary strength is the careful isolation of language-variety adaptation effects through within-family comparisons. A minor weakness is the conflicting results across different metrics which suggests our understanding of how LLM probabilities map to human cognitive load during reading is still incomplete.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How do gaze behaviors and landmark preferences differ between individuals with good and poor sense of direction (SOD)? Furthermore, can a mixed reality system that highlights these landmarks improve spatial learning for poor SOD users?

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over helping users build cognitive maps. This work bridges spatial cognition research and mixed reality interfaces. It specifically targets the gap in spatial learning aids for people with a poor sense of direction by leveraging eye-tracking and vision-language models.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is LandmarkLens, a mixed reality navigation system that uses a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to dynamically identify and highlight navigation relevant landmarks. The authors also provide empirical evidence that guided landmark attention improves scene recognition and spatial knowledge acquisition for users with poor spatial abilities.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a VR study with 20 participants to track gaze behavior and verbal landmark descriptions across different sense of direction abilities. Using these eye-tracking insights, they developed LandmarkLens, which employs a VLM to predict and visually highlight effective landmarks in a mixed reality environment. They then evaluated the system with eight poor SOD participants to measure improvements in scene recognition.

5. Limitations
The evaluation relies on a very small sample size of eight poor SOD participants, which limits the statistical power of the findings. The study also focuses primarily on landmark level spatial knowledge, leaving the impact on broader cognitive map formation untested. Additionally, the initial gaze data collection was restricted to specific Tokyo neighborhoods in VR, which may not generalize globally.

6. Critical Evaluation
The integration of eye-tracking insights with a VLM to create a targeted mixed reality interface is a highly innovative approach to spatial learning. A notable strength is the use of empirical gaze data to inform the VLM highlighting criteria. However, the transition from VR gaze data collection to a mixed reality evaluation creates a potential modality gap, and future work will need to validate the VLM landmark selection in real time on a larger scale to prove true cognitive map improvement.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question

How can visual search behavior, specifically 3D gaze patterns, be used to predict the expertise level of radiologists interpreting volumetric CT scans? The paper aims to solve the problem of objective, process-based expertise assessment in radiology.

2. Positioning

Prior work in medical imaging has extensively used eye-tracking for 2D images but has not effectively extended this to volumetric CT interpretation. This research fills the gap by bringing visual search behavior analysis into the domain of 3D medical image reading. It connects the traditionally separate fields of gaze tracking and advanced transformer based computer vision for skill assessment.

3. Key Contribution

The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise directly from 3D CT reading sessions. The model innovates by integrating raw gaze fixation patterns into a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone using a learnable log-space attention bias and gaze-weighted patch pooling. This provides a new objective metric for evaluating medical training and skill progression.

4. Methodology

The authors utilize a DINOv2 backbone to process volumetric CT data and integrate 3D gaze data through two specific mechanisms. They apply a learnable log-space bias within the self-attention layers to guide the model toward fixated regions, and they perform gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings to aggregate features based on where the radiologist looked. The model was trained on 182 CT reading sessions from five radiologists of varying experience levels.

5. Limitations

The study is limited by a relatively small dataset comprising only 182 reading sessions from five radiologists, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the framework relies on specialized eye-tracking hardware that may not be readily available in all clinical settings. The model also focuses specifically on thoracic CT, so its applicability to other imaging modalities or anatomical regions remains untested.

6. Critical Evaluation

The work is highly promising because it successfully bridges 3D eye-tracking data with modern transformer architectures like DINOv2. Achieving an ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and an F1 score of \(0.86\) demonstrates strong predictive power for a behavioral assessment task. A weakness is the heavy reliance on custom gaze integration, which might require significant retraining to adapt to new clinical tasks or different tracking hardware.
