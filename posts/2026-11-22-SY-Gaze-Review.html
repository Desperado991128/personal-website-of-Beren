Here are selected research papers at the intersection of gaze/eye-tracking and LLMs for November 22, 2026.

Daily Concept: Cognitive load measurement via eye tracking

1. What is it?
Cognitive load measurement via eye tracking is the process of quantifying the mental effort a user exerts while processing information by analyzing their eye movements. In the context of language models, it involves using gaze metrics to assess how difficult it is for a human to read, comprehend, or interact with AI-generated text. This transforms subjective feelings of difficulty into objective physiological signals.

2. How does it work?
The mechanism relies on tracking specific oculomotor features that correlate with mental workload, such as pupil diameter, fixation duration, and blink rate. When a user reads complex text generated by an LLM, their cognitive processing demands increase, leading to measurable changes in these gaze features. A machine learning model can then map these temporal sequences of eye movements to a continuous load score \( L \). For example, a regression model might compute cognitive load as \( L = \alpha \cdot \text{FixationDuration} + \beta \cdot \text{PupilDilation} \), though modern approaches use deep recurrent networks to capture temporal gaze dynamics.

3. Why does it matter?
This measurement is crucial for evaluating and improving human-LLM interaction because it provides implicit feedback on text comprehensibility. By detecting when a user is cognitively overloaded, AI systems can dynamically adjust their output, such as simplifying vocabulary or breaking down long explanations. It enables the development of adaptive interfaces that optimize human understanding and trust when interacting with complex language models.

4. Key insight
Eye tracking provides a direct window into the cognitive cost of processing LLM outputs, allowing AI to adapt to human mental limits in real time. A language model is only as effective as a human's ability to comprehend it, and gaze data quantifies that comprehension bottleneck.

Paper Summaries

Persona Lenses: What Makes a Vision-Language Model See Through Its Persona's Eyes?

Authors: Yoonho Shin
arXiv: 2611.25508v1

Summary

1. Research Question. How effectively does a persona in a prompt influence the simulated gaze of a vision-language model? The paper aims to solve the problem of weak persona influence on model-generated scanpaths by measuring and amplifying this effect.

2. Positioning. Prior work uses vision-language models to simulate user gaze on web pages but struggles to fully align the model's attention with specific user personas. This work fills the gap between prompt-based persona definition and actual visual behavior simulation by introducing methods to actively enforce persona-driven gaze.

3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is the introduction of persona lenses that act as intermediaries between the model and the visual input to steer simulated gaze. The paper proposes three distinct lenses. These are a text lens for intermediate reasoning, a pixel lens for visual marking, and a weight lens using a gated dwell reward to maintain natural scanpaths.

4. Methodology. The authors evaluate Qwen3-VL models (4B and 32B) and Qwen3.5-9B on generated and live web pages. They measure the reach of a persona by comparing gaze differences between MatrAIx personas that differ by a single attribute. They then apply the three proposed lenses to evaluate how well each method shifts the simulated scanpath according to persona interests.

5. Limitations. The text lens is sensitive to irrelevant profile changes, meaning it can overfit to non-gaze attributes. The pixel lens provides weaker persona separation. Furthermore, larger models like the 32B do not benefit from these lenses as they can separate personas with the base prompt alone, limiting the general applicability of the lenses across model scales.

6. Critical Evaluation. This work offers a highly practical framework for aligning VLM gaze simulation with specific user characteristics, a crucial step for believable synthetic eye-tracking data. The trade-off analysis between robustness and separation strength across different lenses and model sizes is a strong point. However, relying on generated pages and specific Qwen architectures might limit the immediate transferability of the weight lens to other VLM families.

The Depth of a Prediction: Where Language Models Resolve a Word Predicts Human Reading Time

Authors: Donato Riccio, Fabrizio Maturo
arXiv: 2611.25970v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Does the internal layer at which a language model resolves a word prediction predict human reading time, beyond what final-layer surprisal already captures?

2. Positioning
Prior work in psycholinguistics and computational linguistics heavily relies on final-layer surprisal from language models to predict human reading times. This paper argues that looking only at the final output ignores the rich internal processing dynamics of deep neural networks. It fills this gap by examining how the layerwise trajectory of a prediction within the model relates to human cognitive processing during reading.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the introduction of "resolution depth", a metric summarizing the specific layer where a word prediction change is centered within a language model. The authors demonstrate that words resolved later in the model, along with the subsequent words, are read more slowly by humans. This metric improves held-out prediction of reading times beyond standard final-layer surprisal and entropy metrics.

4. Methodology
The authors apply the tuned lens to extract word predictions at every layer of ten models from the GPT-2, OPT, and Pythia families. They calculate layerwise surprisal trajectories and extract the resolution depth for each word. They then fit regression models on eight eye-tracking and self-paced reading corpora containing 81,957 words to evaluate how well resolution depth predicts reading times compared to baseline surprisal metrics.

5. Limitations
The net predictive gain from resolution depth varies significantly across different reading corpora, showing a weak random-effects mean of \(0.07\) millinats per word (\(p = 0.37\)) in the baseline comparison. The improvement is also inconsistent across model families, with gains mostly driven by specific corpora within GPT-2 and Pythia. Furthermore, the effect relies on trained models, as untrained networks show no predictive gain.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work offers a compelling bridge between the internal dynamics of deep language models and human cognitive processing, moving beyond superficial final-layer outputs. The use of the tuned lens to extract resolution depth is an elegant way to summarize complex layerwise trajectories. However, the inconsistent gains across corpora and models suggest that the link between layer depth and human reading time is not yet fully robust or universally applicable across all architectures.
