Daily Concept: Eye tracking in multimodal AI

1. What is it?
Eye tracking in multimodal AI refers to the integration of human gaze data into artificial intelligence systems that process multiple data types like text, images, and video. By capturing where a person is looking, these models gain an additional signal of human attention and intent. This allows large language models and vision language models to reason about human visual focus alongside the primary task inputs.

2. How does it work?
The mechanism involves projecting raw gaze coordinates, typically represented as 2D spatial points, into an embedding space that a neural network can process. These gaze sequences are often encoded using temporal models like recurrent neural networks or transformers to capture the dynamics of visual attention. The resulting gaze embeddings are then fused with visual and textual features using cross attention mechanisms. For instance, a multimodal model might concatenate or cross attend to a gaze sequence \( G = \{(x_t, y_t, p_t)\}_{t=1}^{T} \) alongside image patches and text tokens to predict an output \( y \).

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating gaze into large language models bridges the gap between passive observation and active human and computer collaboration. It enables applications like gaze contingent image generation, assistive technologies for users with motor impairments, and more accurate visual question answering by guiding the model to human relevant regions. Furthermore, gaze data serves as a powerful supervision signal, allowing models to learn human aligned attention patterns and improve their interpretability.

4. Key insight
Eye tracking transforms human visual attention into a computable modality that directly guides multimodal reasoning. By teaching AI where humans look, we create systems that understand not just what is presented but what is perceived.

Paper Summaries

Persona Lenses: What Makes a Vision-Language Model See Through Its Persona's Eyes?

Authors: Yoonho Shin
arXiv: 2611.25508v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper investigates how effectively a persona prompt influences the simulated gaze of a vision-language model on a web page. It asks what methods can bridge the gap between a textual persona and the model's actual visual attention output.

2. Positioning
Prior work uses vision-language models to simulate user gaze on web pages, but textual personas only partially reach the simulated output. This work fills the gap by quantifying this disconnect and proposing methods to better align the model's visual predictions with specific persona attributes.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the introduction of persona lenses. These are techniques placed between the model and the web page to enforce persona-driven gaze. The paper presents three distinct lenses including a text lens, a pixel lens, and a weight lens, and demonstrates how their effectiveness depends on model size and computational cost.

4. Methodology
The authors introduce three persona lenses. The text lens uses a two-step prompt where the model first describes the persona's gaze targets. The pixel lens visually marks regions of interest on the page, while the weight lens applies a dwell reward gated to maintain natural scanpaths. They test these approaches using Qwen3-VL 4B and 32B, plus Qwen3.5-9B, on generated and live web pages by measuring the gaze difference between personas that differ in a single attribute.

5. Limitations
The text lens is sensitive to irrelevant profile changes, meaning it can alter gaze based on attributes that should not matter. The pixel lens provides weaker persona separation, and the weight lens requires training. Additionally, larger models like the 32B variant do not require these lenses at all, limiting the applicability of these methods to smaller models.

6. Critical Evaluation
The paper provides a highly practical breakdown of how to control VLM gaze simulation across different model scales. A major strength is the nuanced finding that larger models naturally internalize personas while smaller models need specific architectural or prompt interventions. However, the reliance on specific Qwen models suggests the findings might not generalize universally to all vision-language architectures.
