Daily Concept: Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction

1. What is it?
Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction refer to the spatial and temporal characteristics of eye movements when users read, evaluate, or interact with outputs from large language models. These patterns include fixations, saccades, and pupil dilation metrics that reveal underlying cognitive processes during AI-assisted tasks. By tracking these movements, researchers can objectively measure how humans allocate attention to AI-generated text.

2. How does it work?
Eye-tracking hardware captures raw gaze coordinates at high frequencies, which are then mapped to specific words or tokens in the LLM output. Researchers extract temporal features like fixation duration \( F_d \) and spatial features like saccade amplitude \( S_a \) to quantify reading behavior. These features can be mathematically represented as a gaze embedding vector \( \mathbf{G} = [F_d, S_a, P_d, \dots] \), where \( P_d \) is pupil dilation. This vector is then aligned with token embeddings from the LLM to train models that predict user states such as cognitive load or comprehension difficulty.

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating gaze data into LLM research provides a physiological ground truth for human cognitive states, which subjective surveys often fail to capture. This allows developers to build adaptive AI systems that dynamically adjust response complexity based on real-time user confusion or engagement. Furthermore, it helps evaluate the interpretability and trustworthiness of AI outputs by showing exactly which parts of a generated explanation trigger human scrutiny.

4. Key insight
Human eye movements serve as a real-time cognitive sensor that can enable LLMs to adapt their outputs to the immediate understanding and attention of the user.

Paper Summaries

Learning to Refer from Estimated Listener Gaze

Authors: Téa Wright, Alane Suhr
arXiv: 2609.14207v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can vision-language models learn to generate more pragmatically optimal referring expressions using implicit listener feedback? Specifically, can estimated human gaze scanpaths serve as a viable learning signal for language generation?

2. Positioning
This work bridges gaze modeling and vision-language research. While prior approaches typically train models using explicit success or failure feedback, this paper fills a gap by leveraging the implicit, real-time cognitive process of a listener. It demonstrates that the incremental comprehension process observed through eye-tracking can be used to optimize language generation.

3. Key Contribution
The primary contribution is a novel reinforcement learning framework that uses a gaze-estimating neural listener to provide token and sequence level rewards for a vision-language speaker model. By translating estimated fixation sequences into learning signals, the authors successfully train models that produce significantly shorter and more accurate referring expressions. This proves that implicit listener gaze is a powerful training signal for LLMs.

4. Methodology
The proposed method uses a speaker policy that samples referring expressions based on an image and a target referent. A neural listener model then estimates human gaze behavior, generating a scanpath of fixations where each fixation aligns with a word in the sampled expression. These fixation sequences and the target referents are converted into rewards to optimize the speaker policy parameters.

5. Limitations
The approach relies on a neural listener to estimate human gaze, meaning the speaker learns from simulated rather than actual real-time human eye-tracking data. This introduces a potential sim-to-real gap where biases in the gaze estimator could negatively impact the speaker. Furthermore, the complexity of aligning specific fixations to individual words might limit scalability to more complex linguistic structures.

6. Critical Evaluation
This research offers a compelling bridge between cognitive science and AI by using gaze as an implicit reward signal for LLM fine-tuning. The performance gains are substantial, reducing sequence length from \(15.4\) to \(4.0\) words while increasing referential success from \(75.2\%\) to \(80.0\%\). Its main weakness is the dependence on a gaze estimation model, which makes the system vulnerable to compounding errors from the gaze predictor.

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
This paper investigates whether an AI model can directly predict which traffic object a driver is looking at using facial and scene features. It questions the traditional approach of estimating an intermediate Point-of-Gaze and mapping it to objects.

2. Positioning
Existing driver gaze estimation studies typically represent gaze as a gaze zone or a Point-of-Gaze vector. While these provide spatial coordinates, they lack semantic meaning regarding what the driver is actually attending to. This work fills the gap by shifting from indirect coordinate mapping to direct object-level gaze prediction in real driving scenarios.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is an end-to-end Transformer based framework called TransGaze-Object that directly predicts driver gaze objects. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset called UD-FSG, which contains synchronized driver face and traffic scene images with object bounding boxes and gaze labels. The framework proves that direct prediction achieves an accuracy of \(60\%\) compared to \(51\%\) for traditional association methods.

4. Methodology
The proposed framework extracts facial features, specifically focusing on iris weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. A Transformer cross attention mechanism computes similarity scores and attention weights between the driver face features and the traffic objects. This mechanism allows the model to directly output the attended object without calculating a 2D gaze coordinate as an intermediate step.

5. Limitations
The abstract does not explicitly state limitations, but the achieved overall accuracy of \(60\%\) suggests significant room for improvement before deployment in safety critical systems. The model likely struggles with edge cases where multiple objects are close together or when lighting conditions degrade facial feature extraction. Additionally, the reliance on predefined object bounding boxes means it cannot detect gaze on uncategorized background elements.

6. Critical Evaluation
The work is a strong step forward in semantic gaze tracking, leveraging Transformer cross attention to bridge the gap between human visual input and scene context. Its primary strength is eliminating the error propagation inherent in two step Point-of-Gaze estimation and object association pipelines. However, the \(60\%\) accuracy indicates that real world variability remains a challenge, meaning the system is currently better suited for analytical research than real time driver assistance.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does training language models specifically on Cantonese improve their ability to predict human reading patterns compared to general-purpose or Mandarin-oriented models?

2. Positioning
Information-theoretic measures derived from autoregressive language models are widely used to model human reading expectations. However, it remains unclear if adapting models to specific language varieties like Cantonese improves psycholinguistic alignment, especially given mixed NLP benchmark results for Cantonese-specific training relative to Mandarin or general models.

3. Key Contribution
This paper provides a comprehensive eye-tracking evaluation of Cantonese-adapted language models by comparing within-family model pairs with varying degrees of Cantonese-specific training. It demonstrates that more extensive Cantonese pretraining and instruction tuning generally leads to a better predictive fit for human reading behavior, though this depends on the specific information-theoretic metric used.

4. Methodology
The researchers used naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data to compare two pairs of models. They contrasted a lightly adapted model (JED351) against its base (CKIP GPT-2 Tiny) and an extensively adapted model (CantoneseLLM-7B) against its base (Qwen2.5-7B). They derived four information-theoretic measures from each model to predict reading behavior. These measures included lexical surprisal, POS surprisal, entropy before the target, and entropy reduction.

5. Limitations
The study evaluates only two pairs of within-family models, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other architectures or scales. Furthermore, the conflicting model rankings across different metrics indicate that the choice of information-theoretic measure heavily influences conclusions about psycholinguistic alignment.

6. Critical Evaluation
This study provides a rigorous and timely bridge between large language model adaptation and psycholinguistic modeling by leveraging naturalistic eye-tracking data. Its primary strength lies in the controlled within-family comparisons that isolate the effect of Cantonese-specific training. However, the metric-dependent rankings reveal a weakness in establishing a universal standard for predicting human reading, suggesting a need for more robust evaluation metrics in future work.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question. How do navigation strategies and landmark attention differ between individuals with good and poor sense of direction (SOD)? Can a mixed-reality system that guides visual attention to effective landmarks improve spatial learning for users with poor SOD?

2. Positioning. Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over the development of cognitive maps for spatial learning. This work bridges spatial cognition research and mixed-reality interfaces by addressing the specific needs of users with poor SOD. It fills a gap in utilizing gaze behavior analysis to inform the design of AI-assisted navigation aids.

3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is LandmarkLens, a mixed-reality navigation system that uses a vision-language model (VLM) to automatically identify and highlight effective landmarks. The work is novel in its data-driven approach, deriving landmark saliency rules directly from the eye-tracking data and verbal feedback of users with varying spatial abilities. It demonstrates that guiding visual attention can improve scene recognition for those with poor navigation skills.

4. Methodology. The researchers first conducted a virtual reality study with 20 participants to track eye movements and collect verbal landmark descriptions across four Tokyo neighborhoods. Using these gaze and speech insights, they developed an MR system powered by a VLM to detect and highlight navigation-relevant landmarks in real time. They then evaluated this system with eight poor SOD participants through a scene recognition task.

5. Limitations. The evaluation study is limited by a small sample size of only eight poor SOD participants. The virtual reality training environment may also not fully capture the complexities and physical constraints of real-world urban navigation. Additionally, the VLM might struggle with dynamic or occluded scenes, potentially limiting real-time reliability in busy urban settings.

6. Critical Evaluation. The integration of eye-tracking data with vision-language models to create a targeted spatial learning tool is a significant strength, offering a highly personalized navigation experience. However, the reliance on a small validation sample and a VR prototype means the real-world efficacy of the MR system remains largely unproven. Future work should test the system in outdoor MR environments to validate its long-term impact on cognitive map formation.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper investigates how visual search behavior, specifically 3D gaze patterns, can be used to predict the expertise level of radiologists during volumetric CT interpretation.

2. Positioning
Prior eye-tracking research in medical imaging has largely focused on 2D images. This leaves a significant gap in understanding visual search behaviors in volumetric 3D settings. The work positions itself at the intersection of medical perception and advanced transformer models to bridge this gap.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise from 3D CT reading sessions. It uniquely integrates human fixation patterns into a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone through two distinct mechanisms. These are a learnable log-space bias in self-attention and gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings.

4. Methodology
The authors utilize a DINOv2 backbone to process volumetric CT data alongside eye-tracking data. They inject 3D radiologist fixation patterns into the transformer architecture using a learnable log-space attention bias and gaze-weighted pooling. The framework was trained and evaluated on 182 CT reading sessions from five radiologists with varying experience levels.

5. Limitations
The study is constrained by a relatively small dataset of 182 reading sessions from only five radiologists. This limited number of subjects may restrict the generalizability of the model to broader clinical populations or different imaging modalities.

6. Critical Evaluation
This research offers a compelling demonstration of how human gaze data can be effectively fused with large-scale vision transformer models. Achieving an ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and an F1 score of \(0.86\) highlights the predictive power of process-based metrics over purely outcome-based assessments. However, the small sample size of radiologists is a notable weakness that future work must address to ensure robust clinical applicability.
