Daily Concept: Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction

1. What is it?
Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction refer to the specific ways humans move their eyes when reading, evaluating, or generating text with Large Language Models. These patterns include fixation durations, saccade trajectories, and pupil dilation changes during cognitive processing. By analyzing these ocular behaviors, researchers can infer human cognitive load, attention allocation, and trust levels during AI-assisted tasks.

2. How does it work?
Eye-tracking hardware captures raw gaze coordinates at high frequencies, which are then segmented into fixations and saccades. Machine learning models process these temporal sequences to predict user states, such as confusion or agreement, often using features like fixation duration \( f_d \) and saccadic amplitude \( s_a \). In the context of LLMs, these gaze features can be aligned with the token sequences generated by the model. Researchers might model the probability of a user fixating on a specific token \( w_i \) as \( P(\text{fixation} \mid w_i, \text{context}) \) to evaluate how the model output structure influences human reading behavior.

3. Why does it matter?
This intersection matters because it provides a passive, continuous feedback loop for evaluating LLM interfaces without interrupting the user. It enables the development of adaptive LLM systems that can dynamically adjust text presentation, complexity, or generation parameters based on real-time user cognitive load. Furthermore, gaze data serves as a behavioral ground truth for human attention, helping researchers fine-tune LLMs to produce outputs that align more naturally with human reading patterns.

4. Key insight
Human gaze serves as a real-time cognitive probe that bridges the gap between static LLM text generation and dynamic human comprehension. By treating eye movements as implicit feedback, we can build AI systems that adapt to human cognitive states rather than forcing humans to adapt to the AI.

Paper Summaries

Learning to Refer from Estimated Listener Gaze

Authors: Téa Wright, Alane Suhr
arXiv: 2609.14207v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we use a listener's implicit comprehension process, specifically their eye gaze, as a learning signal to train vision-language models to generate more efficient and pragmatically optimal referring expressions?

2. Positioning
Traditional language generation models typically learn from explicit signals of communicative success or failure. This work fills a gap in the existing literature by leveraging implicit, real-time observations of human comprehension. It bridges psycholinguistics and LLM alignment by using gaze scanpaths as a continuous feedback mechanism during model training.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel reinforcement learning framework that transforms estimated listener gaze fixations into token- and sequence-level rewards for training a speaker policy. By aligning fixations with words in a referring expression, the model learns to produce significantly shorter and more accurate references. The approach reduces sequence length from \(15.4\) to \(4.0\) words while increasing referential success from \(75.2\%\) to \(80.0\%\).

4. Methodology
The proposed method fine-tunes a vision-language model acting as a speaker by sampling referring expressions conditioned on an image and a target referent. A neural listener model estimates human gaze behavior by generating a scanpath of fixations for each word in the sampled expression. These fixation sequences and the target referent are then converted into reward signals to optimize the speaker policy parameters.

5. Limitations
The paper relies on a neural listener to estimate human gaze, which may not perfectly capture the complexity and variability of actual human eye-tracking behavior. Additionally, the approach is currently evaluated only on referring expression generation in static visual scenes. The reliance on estimated gaze rather than ground-truth, real-time gaze data might constrain the fidelity of the comprehension signal.

6. Critical Evaluation
A major strength of this work is its innovative use of implicit gaze signals as a reward mechanism for LLMs, offering a highly efficient alternative to explicit human feedback. The quantitative improvements are compelling, demonstrating a drastic reduction in utterance length without sacrificing accuracy. However, a notable weakness is the dependence on a surrogate gaze estimator, meaning the model learns from a simulation of human attention rather than true human eye-tracking data.

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question. The paper aims to predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at, rather than just estimating a gaze point or zone. The core research question asks whether directly predicting a gaze object from driver face and traffic scene data is more accurate than estimating a Point-of-Gaze and mapping it to objects.

2. Positioning. Existing driver gaze estimation studies typically represent gaze as a geometric gaze vector, a Point-of-Gaze, or a predefined gaze zone. This work argues that object-level gaze provides a more semantically meaningful representation of visual attention. It fills the gap of lacking end-to-end models that directly link driver facial features to specific traffic objects in real driving scenarios.

3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is the TransGaze-Object framework, an end-to-end Transformer based model that directly predicts driver gaze objects. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset called Urban Driving-Face Scene Gaze (UD-FSG) containing synchronized driver face and traffic scene images with object bounding boxes and gaze labels.

4. Methodology. The framework extracts facial features, specifically face and iris-weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. It employs a Transformer cross-attention mechanism to compute similarity scores and attention weights between the driver features and traffic objects. This allows the model to directly output the attended object without intermediate Point-of-Gaze estimation.

5. Limitations. The abstract reports an overall accuracy of \(60\%\), which suggests there is still significant room for improvement in complex real-world driving environments. The model likely struggles with small or distant objects and relies heavily on the quality of object detection bounding boxes. Furthermore, the proposed dataset and evaluation are limited to urban driving scenarios.

6. Critical Evaluation. The work is highly practical, leveraging Transformer cross-attention to bridge the gap between low-level facial features and high-level semantic scene understanding. Its strength lies in bypassing the error-prone intermediate step of Point-of-Gaze estimation. However, a \(60\%\) absolute accuracy indicates that robust deployment in safety-critical advanced driver assistance systems remains a challenge.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does language-variety-specific training in autoregressive language models improve their ability to predict human reading behavior? Specifically, the paper investigates whether Cantonese-adapted LLMs align better with human gaze patterns than their non-adapted counterparts.

2. Positioning
Information-theoretic measures from LLMs are commonly used to model human reading expectations, but the benefit of dialect-specific training remains unclear. For Cantonese, previous NLP benchmarks show mixed results regarding the value of Cantonese-specific adaptation over general Mandarin or multilingual models. This work fills the gap by directly evaluating these models against naturalistic human eye-tracking data rather than standard NLP benchmarks.

3. Key Contribution
The paper provides an empirical evaluation of how different degrees of Cantonese-specific continued pretraining affect psycholinguistic alignment. It demonstrates that more extensive adaptation (as seen in CantoneseLLM-7B) generally yields a stronger fit with human reading data, particularly for lexical surprisal. Furthermore, it highlights that model rankings fluctuate depending on the specific information-theoretic metric used, such as entropy reduction favoring a smaller, lightly adapted model.

4. Methodology
The authors compare two pairs of models with varying degrees of Cantonese adaptation (CKIP GPT-2 Tiny versus JED351, and Qwen2.5-7B versus CantoneseLLM-7B) using naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data. From each model, they extract four information-theoretic metrics including lexical surprisal (\(S_{w}\)), POS surprisal, pre-target entropy (\(H_{pre}\)), and entropy reduction (\(\Delta H\)). These metrics are then statistically evaluated to see how well they predict human gaze behavior during reading.

5. Limitations
The study is limited to only two pairs of models, which may not represent the full landscape of Cantonese or Chinese language models. Additionally, the findings suggest that model performance is highly metric-dependent, meaning a single unified conclusion about optimal psycholinguistic alignment is difficult to establish. The evaluation also relies on specific eye-tracking datasets, which may have their own constraints regarding participant size or text diversity.

6. Critical Evaluation
A major strength of this work is its direct bridge between NLP model adaptation and psycholinguistic eye-tracking research, moving beyond standard NLP evaluations. The nuanced finding that metric choice dictates model ranking is highly valuable for cognitive modeling. However, a weakness is the narrow selection of model families tested, leaving open questions about how these findings scale to other architectures or parameter sizes.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How do navigation strategies and landmark attention differ based on spatial ability. Can a mixed-reality system using a vision-language model improve spatial learning for people with a poor sense of direction.

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over spatial learning, leaving users with a poor sense of direction unable to build effective cognitive maps. This work bridges spatial cognition research and AI-assisted navigation by translating gaze and behavioral differences into a real-time mixed-reality interface.

3. Key Contribution
The paper introduces LandmarkLens, a mixed-reality navigation system that leverages a vision-language model to identify and highlight navigation-relevant landmarks. It provides empirical evidence that guided landmark attention, derived from gaze-behavior insights, helps poor-SOD individuals acquire spatial knowledge.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a VR study with 20 participants, using eye-tracking to compare gaze behavior and landmark preferences between good and poor SOD groups. They then built LandmarkLens using a VLM to detect effective landmarks based on these insights. A follow-up study with eight poor-SOD participants evaluated the system by measuring scene recognition improvements.

5. Limitations
The follow-up study is relatively small with only eight poor-SOD participants, which may limit the statistical power of the findings. The reliance on VR for the initial gaze study and MR for the evaluation might not perfectly translate to real-world urban environments. Lighting and physical obstacles could also affect VLM performance in the wild.

6. Critical Evaluation
The integration of eye-tracking insights with a VLM to drive mixed-reality interfaces is a strong and novel application of multimodal AI. The small sample size of the evaluation phase and potential latency of VLMs in real-time MR are weaknesses that need addressing before widespread deployment.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question. The paper aims to determine whether a radiologist's level of expertise can be predicted from their visual search behavior during 3D CT interpretation. The core question is how to effectively combine eye-tracking data with volumetric image features to objectively assess radiology skills.

2. Positioning. Prior eye-tracking research in medical imaging has primarily focused on 2D images. This work addresses the underexplored area of 3D volumetric CT scans. It fills the gap by moving beyond simple image analysis to model the cognitive process of navigating and interpreting 3D medical volumes.

3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D gaze patterns. It introduces a new way to fuse eye-tracking data with visual features by modifying the transformer architecture itself rather than just treating gaze as an external input.

4. Methodology. The approach uses a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone to process volumetric CT data. Gaze data is integrated through two mechanisms. First, a learnable log-space bias is added to the self-attention mechanism. Second, gaze-weighted pooling is applied to the patch embeddings to emphasize diagnostically relevant regions.

5. Limitations. The evaluation relies on a relatively small dataset of 182 reading sessions from only five radiologists. This limited number of human subjects may restrict the model's ability to generalize to a broader population of readers. Additionally, tracking 3D gaze accurately in volumetric scans presents inherent technical challenges that could introduce noise.

6. Critical Evaluation. The work is a strong step forward in process-based medical AI, demonstrating high predictive performance with an \( \text{ROC-AUC} = 0.91 \) and \( \text{F1} = 0.86 \). The architectural choice to inject gaze directly into the self-attention bias and pooling of a DINOv2 model is technically sound and elegant. However, the reliance on such a small cohort of radiologists is a notable weakness that raises questions about the robustness and scalability of the expertise classifier.
