Here are selected research papers at the intersection of gaze/eye-tracking and LLMs for September 20, 2026.

Daily Concept: Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction

1. What is it? Gaze patterns in human-AI interaction refer to the characteristic ways humans visually scan AI-generated text, interfaces, or multimodal outputs. In the context of large language models, this involves tracking eye movements to understand cognitive load, reading comprehension, or user intent during human-AI collaboration. It also includes the reverse, where AI models use gaze data as an input modality to adapt or personalize responses.

2. How does it work? Eye-tracking hardware captures raw ocular features like fixations, saccades, and pupil dilation, which are then mapped to spatial coordinates on a screen displaying LLM outputs. These spatial coordinates are aligned with tokenized text, creating a multimodal dataset where visual attention is quantified. A machine learning model can then process this gaze data using attention mechanisms, where a gaze weight \( g_i \) is assigned to a specific token embedding \( e_i \). The updated representation \( h_i = e_i + \alpha g_i \) allows the LLM to dynamically adjust its generation based on where the user is looking or their inferred cognitive state.

3. Why does it matter? Integrating gaze patterns into LLM research provides a continuous, implicit feedback signal that reveals user confusion, interest, or cognitive load without requiring explicit text prompts. This allows for the development of adaptive AI systems that can dynamically adjust their reading difficulty, summarization length, or conversational pacing in real time. Ultimately, it bridges the gap between human cognitive states and machine understanding, pushing toward more intuitive and collaborative human-AI interfaces.

4. Key insight Human gaze provides a real-time, continuous proxy for cognitive state, enabling LLMs to align their outputs with human attention and comprehension dynamically.

Paper Summaries

Learning to Refer from Estimated Listener Gaze

Authors: Téa Wright, Alane Suhr
arXiv: 2609.14207v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Can a vision-language model learn to generate more pragmatically optimal referring expressions by observing a listener's implicit comprehension process? This paper investigates whether estimated human gaze scanpaths can serve as a learning signal to optimize speaker policies.

2. Positioning
Traditional approaches to training language models rely primarily on explicit signals of communicative success or failure. This work fills a gap by leveraging implicitly available observations of a listener's real time comprehension process. It positions itself at the intersection of pragmatics, vision language modeling, and eye tracking research.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel reinforcement learning framework that finetunes vision-language models using gaze scanpaths as rewards. By training a neural listener to estimate human gaze, the authors create a feedback loop that improves the speaker's referring expressions. This approach successfully reduces utterance length from \(15.4\) to \(4.0\) words while increasing referential success from \(75.2\%\) to \(80.0\%\).

4. Methodology
The authors sample referring expressions from a speaker policy conditioned on images and target referents. A neural listener model then estimates human gaze behavior by mapping these expressions and images to scanpaths, representing fixations aligned to specific words. These fixation sequences are converted into token level and sequence level rewards to optimize the speaker policy parameters.

5. Limitations
The paper does not explicitly detail the potential domain shift between estimated gaze and actual human gaze, which could propagate errors during training. Additionally, the approach relies heavily on the neural listener accurately modeling complex human cognitive processes. The evaluation is also restricted to referring expression generation, leaving broader conversational applicability untested.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is highly innovative in its use of eye tracking data as an implicit reward signal for language generation. The dramatic reduction in sequence length with maintained or improved accuracy demonstrates a strong grasp of pragmatic efficiency. However, the reliance on an estimated gaze model rather than real time human gaze during training might limit the upper bound of pragmatic optimization.

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we accurately predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at, such as a pedestrian or traffic signal, without relying on intermediate Point-of-Gaze (PoG) estimation?

2. Positioning
Previous gaze tracking research in driving contexts typically estimates visual attention using broad gaze zones or continuous Point-of-Gaze (PoG) coordinates. This work argues that object-level gaze is more semantically meaningful. It fills the gap of directly mapping driver visual attention to scene objects rather than relying on error-prone post-hoc associations between a PoG and detected objects.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is TransGaze-Object, an end-to-end Transformer based framework that directly predicts driver gaze objects from facial and traffic scene features. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset, Urban Driving-Face Scene Gaze (UD-FSG), which provides synchronized face and scene images with object bounding boxes and gaze labels.

4. Methodology
The framework extracts driver facial features, specifically weighting the eye and iris regions, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. A Transformer cross-attention mechanism computes similarity scores and attention weights between the driver state and scene objects to directly output the attended object.

5. Limitations
The paper reports a moderate overall accuracy of \(60\%\), meaning the model still misclassifies the attended object a significant portion of the time. Furthermore, the reliance on a newly introduced dataset suggests the framework might need extensive validation across diverse driving conditions and environments not covered in UD-FSG.

6. Critical Evaluation
The direct prediction approach is a strong conceptual shift away from traditional PoG pipelines, effectively reducing background confusion by almost \(50\%\). However, an accuracy of \(60\%\) indicates substantial room for improvement before deployment in safety critical Advanced Driver Assistance Systems.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question. Does adapting language models specifically for Cantonese improve their ability to predict human reading patterns compared to general-purpose or Mandarin-oriented models? The paper aims to determine if language-variety-specific training enhances psycholinguistic alignment with human gaze data.

2. Positioning. Previous research uses information-theoretic measures from language models to model human reading expectations. However, it remains unclear if training models on specific language varieties like Cantonese improves this alignment. This is a notable gap because recent NLP evaluations have shown mixed results for Cantonese-specific models.

3. Key Contribution. The paper provides an empirical evaluation of how Cantonese-specific continued pretraining affects eye-tracking gaze prediction. It demonstrates that more extensive Cantonese adaptation generally yields a stronger predictive fit for human reading behavior. It also reveals that model rankings fluctuate depending on the specific information-theoretic metric used.

4. Methodology. The authors compare two pairs of within-family models using naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data. They extract four information-theoretic measures from each model, specifically lexical surprisal, POS surprisal, entropy before the target, and entropy reduction. These metrics are then evaluated against the human gaze data to determine which model best predicts reading patterns.

5. Limitations. The study is constrained by its reliance on only two pairs of within-family model contrasts. This may not capture the full landscape of Cantonese language modeling. Furthermore, the conflicting results across different metrics suggest that the theoretical mapping between model probabilities and cognitive processing is still incomplete.

6. Critical Evaluation. This work is highly valuable for bridging NLP and psycholinguistics by directly evaluating language-variety-specific training against human gaze data. A major strength is the use of within-family model comparisons to isolate the impact of Cantonese adaptation. A weakness is the metric-dependent nature of the results, which complicates the establishment of a single definitive ranking for psycholinguistic alignment.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How do gaze behaviors and landmark preferences differ between individuals with good and poor sense of direction (SOD), and can a mixed-reality system using AI improve spatial learning for poor-SOD users?

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools focus on getting users to a destination quickly rather than helping them build cognitive maps for spatial learning. This work bridges the gap between spatial cognition research and AI-assisted navigation by using eye-tracking and verbal reports to understand navigational difficulties. It introduces a mixed-reality approach that actively guides visual attention to foster spatial memory.

3. Key Contribution
The paper introduces LandmarkLens, a mixed-reality navigation system that leverages a vision-language model (VLM) to identify and highlight effective landmarks. The main novelty lies in using empirical eye-tracking and cognitive data to inform an AI-driven interface that actively supports spatial knowledge acquisition rather than just route efficiency. The authors demonstrate that this guided visual attention improves scene recognition for users with poor navigational abilities.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a VR navigation study with 20 participants, tracking their gaze behavior and verbal landmark descriptions across four Tokyo neighborhoods. Based on the distinct gaze patterns and landmark preferences of poor versus good SOD groups, they developed LandmarkLens. This system uses a VLM to detect and highlight navigation-relevant landmarks in a mixed-reality interface, which was then tested on eight poor-SOD participants to evaluate scene recognition improvements.

5. Limitations
The evaluation study involves a relatively small sample size of only eight poor-SOD participants, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the results. Additionally, the VR navigation study and MR evaluation might not fully capture the complexities and physical motions of real-world urban exploration. The paper also does not detail the specific computational costs or latency of the VLM in a real-time mixed-reality setting.

6. Critical Evaluation
The integration of eye-tracking insights with a VLM to enhance spatial cognition is highly innovative and addresses a genuine accessibility gap in navigation tools. However, the reliance on a small sample size for the final evaluation leaves questions about how robust these effects are across diverse populations and varied urban topologies. Future work should explore real-time deployment constraints and long-term spatial knowledge retention outside of virtual environments.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper aims to determine whether a radiologist's level of expertise can be predicted from their 3D gaze patterns during volumetric CT interpretation. The core research question asks how visual search behavior can be integrated into deep learning models to objectively assess radiology skills.

2. Positioning
While eye-tracking is well established in 2D medical imaging research, its application to 3D volumetric CT scans for assessing expertise remains largely unexplored. This work bridges the gap between traditional 2D gaze analysis and modern transformer architectures by applying visual search behavior analysis to 3D CT reading. It positions gaze data not just as a diagnostic tool but as a ground truth signal for skill assessment.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D CT eye-tracking data. The authors introduce two specific innovations to integrate fixation patterns into a DINOv2 backbone. These are a learnable log-space bias in the self-attention mechanism and a gaze-weighted pooling approach for patch embeddings.

4. Methodology
The authors utilize a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone to process volumetric CT scans and incorporate radiologist fixation patterns directly into the model. They implement a learnable log-space bias to guide the self-attention layers based on gaze coordinates and apply gaze-weighted pooling to aggregate patch embeddings. The framework was trained and evaluated on a dataset of 182 CT reading sessions from five radiologists with varying experience levels.

5. Limitations
The primary limitation is the relatively small sample size of only five radiologists and 182 reading sessions, which may restrict the generalizability of the model to broader clinical populations. Additionally, the model relies on specific 3D CT hardware and eye-tracking setups, potentially limiting its deployment in standard clinical environments without specialized equipment. The classification of expertise might also oversimplify the continuous nature of real-world skill acquisition.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work presents a compelling integration of gaze-tracking data into modern vision transformers, achieving high predictive performance with an \( \text{ROC-AUC} = 0.91 \) and \( F1 = 0.86 \). The technical approach of modifying self-attention with log-space gaze biases is an elegant way to fuse human visual search behavior with machine learning. However, the practical utility of the model will remain limited until it can be validated on a larger, more diverse cohort of readers and scanning protocols.
