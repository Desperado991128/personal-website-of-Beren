Here are selected research papers at the intersection of gaze/eye-tracking and LLMs for November 21, 2026.

Daily Concept: Eye tracking in multimodal AI

1. What is it?
Eye tracking in multimodal AI involves integrating human gaze data as an additional input modality alongside text or images within artificial intelligence systems. It captures where a person is looking in a visual scene and feeds this spatial and temporal attention data into models like Large Language Models. This allows the AI to ground its language generation and reasoning in human visual attention patterns.

2. How does it work?
Gaze data is typically represented as a sequence of 2D fixation points or heatmaps over time. These coordinates are encoded into continuous embeddings using a gaze encoder that projects them into the same latent space as visual or text tokens. The multimodal model then uses cross-attention mechanisms to fuse these gaze embeddings with other modalities. For example, the fused attention weight \( A \) for a visual patch \( v_i \) might be calculated as \( A(v_i) = \alpha \cdot A_{text}(v_i) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot A_{gaze}(v_i) \), where \( \alpha \) is a learned weighting parameter.

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating eye tracking into LLMs bridges the gap between passive text generation and active human-computer interaction. It enables AI systems to predict user intent and align responses with what the user is currently focusing on. Furthermore, gaze data serves as a powerful supervisory signal for training more sample-efficient vision-language models by mimicking human cognitive attention.

4. Key insight
Human gaze acts as a direct interface for AI, translating implicit cognitive attention into explicit computational guidance. By reading where we look, LLMs can understand what we care about without requiring explicit text prompts.

Paper Summaries

Persona Lenses: What Makes a Vision-Language Model See Through Its Persona's Eyes?

Authors: Yoonho Shin
arXiv: 2611.25508v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How effectively can a text persona in a prompt influence a vision language model's simulated gaze on a web page? The core research question asks how to reliably bridge the gap between a defined persona and the model's visual attention output.

2. Positioning
Prior work uses vision language models to simulate user gaze on web pages but finds that prompt personas only partly influence the resulting scanpath. This research fills the gap by systematically measuring how individual persona attributes alter model gaze. It introduces explicit methods to enforce a stronger connection between the defined persona and the visual attention output.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the introduction of persona lenses that mediate the interaction between the persona, the vision language model, and the web page. The paper proposes three distinct lenses. These include a text lens for explicit reasoning, a pixel lens for visual marking, and a weight lens using a gated dwell reward to maintain natural scanpaths.

4. Methodology
The authors evaluate Qwen3 VL models (\(4B\) and \(32B\)) and Qwen3.5 \(9B\) on generated and live web pages. They measure the gaze difference between MatrAIx personas that differ by exactly one attribute. They then compare the performance of the three proposed lenses against each other and against a baseline of simply scaling up the model to \(32B\) without any lens.

5. Limitations
The text lens is sensitive to irrelevant profile changes, meaning it can alter gaze when it should not. The pixel lens is more robust but separates personas less effectively. Additionally, the necessity of these lenses diminishes with larger models, as the \(32B\) model achieves persona separation with the base prompt alone.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work provides a highly practical breakdown of how to control gaze simulation in vision language models across different model scales. The trade-offs between robustness, separation strength, and compute cost are clearly articulated. A weakness is the reliance on specific Qwen architectures, which might limit the generalizability of the trained weight lens to other model families.

The Depth of a Prediction: Where Language Models Resolve a Word Predicts Human Reading Time

Authors: Donato Riccio, Fabrizio Maturo
arXiv: 2611.25970v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Does the internal processing depth at which a language model resolves a word prediction influence human reading times. Specifically, can looking at where a prediction is formed inside the model tell us more about human reading behavior than just the final prediction error.

2. Positioning
Surprisal theory traditionally links human reading times to the prediction error from the final layer of a language model. This work extends that theory by examining the internal layer-by-layer dynamics of the model. It bridges cognitive science and mechanistic interpretability to understand if the timing of a prediction inside a neural network mirrors human cognitive processing.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the introduction of "resolution depth" which measures the specific layer where a language model finalizes a word prediction. The authors demonstrate that words resolved later in the model and the subsequent words are read more slowly by humans. This metric improves held-out prediction of human reading times beyond standard final-layer surprisal by \(0.26\) millinats per word.

4. Methodology
The authors use the tuned lens technique to extract word predictions at every layer of ten models from the GPT-2, OPT, and Pythia families. They compute layerwise surprisal trajectories and extract the resolution depth. They then evaluate this metric against eight English eye-tracking and self-paced reading corpora containing \(81,957\) words to predict human reading times.

5. Limitations
The net gain from resolution depth varies across different reading corpora and is not always statistically significant in standard random-effects models. The effect is specific to trained networks and disappears when applying a basic logit lens to untrained models. Additionally the metric captures most of the trajectory information but a whole-trajectory functional regression shows no further gains.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work offers a compelling psycholinguistic insight by showing that human reading times are sensitive to the internal computational trajectory of language models. A major strength is the rigorous evaluation across multiple eye-tracking corpora and model families. However the inconsistent statistical significance across corpora suggests that the relationship between layerwise model processing and human cognition is complex and potentially noisy.
