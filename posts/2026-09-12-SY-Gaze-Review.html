Here are selected research papers at the intersection of gaze/eye-tracking and LLMs for September 12, 2026.

Daily Concept: Cognitive load measurement via eye tracking

1. What is it?
Cognitive load measurement via eye tracking is the process of quantifying the mental effort a user exerts by analyzing their eye movements. In the context of LLMs, it provides a physiological proxy for how difficult it is for a human to read or interact with model generated text. This allows researchers to evaluate AI systems based on human cognitive strain rather than just task accuracy.

2. How does it work?
Eye trackers capture high frequency data on pupil diameter, fixation durations, and saccadic movements. Increased cognitive demand often correlates with pupil dilation and longer fixation times on specific tokens or interface elements. Machine learning models process these gaze features to predict a continuous cognitive load score. For instance, a regression model might map a feature vector \( \mathbf{X} \) of gaze metrics to a load estimate \( \hat{y} = f(\mathbf{X}) \), where \( \mathbf{X} \) includes features like average fixation duration \( F_{avg} \) and pupil diameter change \( \Delta D \).

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating cognitive load metrics into LLM evaluation creates a human focused feedback loop for model optimization. Developers can use gaze data to identify when an LLM generates overly complex or confusing outputs, enabling them to fine tune prompts or model parameters for better readability. Ultimately, this bridges the gap between algorithmic performance and human comprehension, ensuring that language models are truly accessible and efficient for users.

4. Key insight
Eye tracking transforms human cognitive strain into measurable data that can guide the training and refinement of language models. The ultimate goal of an LLM should not just be correct text generation, but generating text that minimizes the cognitive load of the human reader.

Paper Summaries

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

1. Research Question
How can we directly predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at in real driving scenarios? The paper aims to move beyond intermediate Point-of-Gaze estimation by directly mapping driver visual attention to semantically meaningful traffic objects.

2. Positioning
Prior research in driver gaze tracking typically estimates gaze zones or a continuous Point-of-Gaze. This work fills a critical gap by shifting focus to object-level gaze prediction, which provides a more semantic understanding of situational awareness without relying on error-prone geometric associations between a 2D point and scene bounding boxes.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the TransGaze-Object framework, an end-to-end Transformer based model that directly predicts driver gaze objects from synchronized facial and traffic scene inputs. Additionally, the authors introduce a new benchmark dataset called Urban Driving-Face Scene Gaze (UD-FSG) containing synchronized driver faces, traffic scenes, and object level gaze labels.

4. Methodology
The model extracts facial features, specifically combining whole face and iris-weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. A Transformer cross-attention mechanism then computes similarity scores and attention weights between the driver features and the traffic objects to predict the attended target.

5. Limitations
While the direct prediction approach improves accuracy, the overall performance of \(60\%\) indicates that object-level gaze prediction in dynamic real-world driving remains highly challenging. The reliance on a single newly proposed dataset might also limit the generalizability of the model across different geographic regions, lighting conditions, or vehicle interiors.

6. Critical Evaluation
The strength of this work lies in its elegant use of Transformer cross-attention to bridge facial cues and scene objects, effectively reducing background confusion by \(49.7\%\) compared to traditional Point-of-Gaze methods. However, the absolute accuracy leaves room for improvement before deployment in safety-critical driver assistance systems, suggesting a need for further refinement in feature extraction or model scaling.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does adapting language models specifically for Cantonese improve their ability to predict human reading behavior compared to general-purpose or Mandarin-oriented models?

2. Positioning
Information-theoretic measures from autoregressive language models are widely used to model human reading expectations. However, it remains unclear if training on a specific language variety like Cantonese improves this psycholinguistic alignment. This is particularly relevant given mixed NLP benchmark results for Cantonese models relative to Mandarin or general-purpose ones.

3. Key Contribution
This paper provides an eye-tracking based evaluation of Cantonese-adapted language models using naturalistic reading data. It demonstrates that more extensive Cantonese-specific pretraining and instruction tuning correlate with a better predictive fit for human reading. The work also highlights that model rankings vary depending on the specific information-theoretic metric being evaluated.

4. Methodology
The authors compare two pairs of within-family models using naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data. They contrast a lightly adapted model (JED351) with its base (CKIP GPT-2 Tiny), and an extensively adapted model (CantoneseLLM-7B) with its base (Qwen2.5-7B). From these models, they extract four metrics to predict reading behavior, specifically lexical surprisal \( S \), POS surprisal, pre-target entropy \( H \), and entropy reduction \( \Delta H \).

5. Limitations
The study is limited by its focus on only two pairs of models, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other architectures or scales of adaptation. Furthermore, the conflicting model rankings across different metrics suggest that the field still lacks a single, unified measure for evaluating psycholinguistic alignment.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is a valuable bridge between psycholinguistics and language model adaptation for low-resource linguistic varieties. Its primary strength lies in the controlled within-family model comparisons, which isolate the effect of Cantonese training. However, the metric-dependent rankings reveal a theoretical weakness, suggesting that \( S \) and \( \Delta H \) might capture fundamentally different cognitive processes during reading.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper investigates how gaze behaviors and landmark preferences differ between individuals with good and poor spatial navigation abilities. It also asks whether a mixed-reality system using an AI model can effectively highlight landmarks to improve spatial learning for those with a poor sense of direction.

2. Positioning
Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over the spatial learning required to build cognitive maps. This work bridges spatial cognition research and mixed-reality interfaces by using gaze tracking to identify behavioral gaps between users with varying navigation abilities. It fills a gap in assistive navigation by translating gaze-based cognitive insights into an automated AI system for spatial learning.

3. Key Contribution
The paper introduces LandmarkLens, a mixed-reality navigation tool that uses a vision-language model to automatically identify and highlight useful navigation landmarks. The main novelty lies in grounding the AI selection process in empirical eye tracking data that reveals how different users visually attend to their environment. This approach demonstrates that guiding visual attention via AI can improve spatial knowledge acquisition for users with poor navigation skills.

4. Methodology
The researchers first conducted a virtual reality study with 20 participants to track eye movements and verbal landmark descriptions across four Tokyo neighborhoods. Using these gaze and linguistic insights, they designed a mixed-reality system that leverages a vision-language model to recognize effective landmarks. They then evaluated this system with eight poor navigation ability users to measure improvements in scene recognition.

5. Limitations
The evaluation study is limited by a small sample size of only eight participants with a poor sense of direction. Additionally, the research relies on virtual reality environments for the initial gaze data collection, which may not perfectly translate to real world visual distractions. The system also focuses on landmark level spatial knowledge, which is just an initial step toward comprehensive cognitive map building.

6. Critical Evaluation
The integration of eye tracking to inform a vision-language model is a highly innovative approach to personalized assistive technology. A major strength is the clear link established between cognitive gaze behaviors and actionable AI outputs in mixed reality. However, the practical deployment of real time VLM inference on lightweight MR hardware remains a technical hurdle that the abstract does not address.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
The paper investigates whether 3D gaze patterns from radiologists during volumetric CT interpretation can be used to accurately predict their level of expertise. It asks how visual search behavior can be integrated into deep learning models to objectively assess radiology skills.

2. Positioning
Previous eye-tracking research in medical imaging has predominantly focused on 2D image analysis. This work addresses the gap in 3D volumetric CT settings by mapping visual search behaviors to skill levels. It bridges the gap between raw eye-tracking data and modern transformer architectures for process-based expertise assessment.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D CT reading sessions. The paper demonstrates how integrating raw fixation data into a vision transformer improves predictive performance over adapted baseline methods.

4. Methodology
The authors use a DINOv2 vision transformer backbone to process volumetric CT data. They integrate radiologist fixation patterns into the model using two techniques. These are a learnable log-space bias in the self-attention mechanism and gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings. The model was trained on 182 reading sessions from five radiologists.

5. Limitations
The study relies on a relatively small dataset of only five radiologists and 182 reading sessions. This limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of the model to broader populations or different clinical specialties. The framework also depends on high fidelity eye-tracking hardware which may not be available in standard clinical workflows.

6. Critical Evaluation
The work is highly innovative in its technical integration of 3D gaze data into a DINOv2 transformer backbone. The strong ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and F1 score of \(0.86\) highlight the robustness of the proposed log-space attention bias and pooling techniques. However, the practical deployment of such a system remains questionable due to the small cohort size and the need for specialized eye-tracking equipment.

Matched Outcomes, Divergent Gaze: How Foveated MLLMs Search Compared to Humans

Authors: Mohamed Amine Kerkouri, Marouane Tliba, Aladine Chetouani et al.
arXiv: 2608.16514v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Do Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) perform visual search like humans when given identical foveated visual inputs? The core question is whether matching a model's retinal input to a human's makes its gaze process and search behavior human-like.

2. Positioning
Prior work evaluating AI vision models often relies on spatial alignment or saliency metrics, which ignore the temporal dynamics of visual search. This paper fills that gap by evaluating MLLMs on the temporal process of looking. It directly compares model scanpaths to human eye movements under matched foveated viewing conditions to see if outcome similarity implies process similarity.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the demonstration that MLLMs can achieve human-level or superior search outcomes without exhibiting human-like gaze processes. The authors identify a critical process gap where models produce low-entropy, self-consistent scanpaths indicative of non-serial, single-pass architectures. This proves that standard answer-alignment and saliency metrics are insufficient to certify human-like vision.

4. Methodology
The authors evaluate three general-purpose MLLMs on the COCO-Search18 dataset using a goal-directed visual search task. They drive each model fixation by fixation through an identical human-matched foveated view, meaning the model only receives high-resolution input where the fovea lands. Performance is then assessed across three distinct axes. These axes are target presence decisions, target acquisition efficiency, and the gaze process itself.

5. Limitations
The study acknowledges that no degradation regime tested could recover human-like search behavior at human-like success rates, suggesting the gap is architectural rather than parameter-based. Additionally, the research is limited to three general-purpose MLLMs and one specific dataset. The generalizability of these findings to other architectures or specialized vision models remains an open question.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work provides a vital contribution to both the eye-tracking and AI communities by exposing the temporal blind spot in current MLLM evaluation metrics. Its strength lies in the rigorous experimental design that isolates foveated input to fairly compare humans and models. However, a weakness is that while it clearly identifies the architectural divergence, it stops short of proposing a concrete solution to bridge the temporal gap between human serial search and non-serial model processing.
