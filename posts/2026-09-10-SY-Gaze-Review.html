Here are selected research papers at the intersection of gaze/eye-tracking and LLMs for September 10, 2026.

Daily Concept: Eye tracking in multimodal AI

1. What is it?
Eye tracking in multimodal AI involves integrating human gaze data as an explicit input modality alongside text and visual data in large language models. It captures where a person is looking on a screen or in a scene to inform the AI about human attention and cognitive intent. This allows the model to align its reasoning processes with human visual grounding.

2. How does it work?
The process begins by recording raw gaze coordinates and converting them into spatial fixation maps. These maps are patchified and projected into continuous embeddings, similar to how image patches are processed in vision transformers. The gaze embeddings \( G \) are then concatenated with visual embeddings \( V \) and text token embeddings \( T \) before entering the transformer layers. The model learns an attention mechanism over this joint representation \( X = [T, V, G] \) to condition its text generation on human regions of interest.

3. Why does it matter?
Integrating gaze into LLMs bridges the gap between human cognitive states and machine reasoning, enabling more intuitive human-AI interaction. It allows models to prioritize salient visual features dynamically, improving computational efficiency and reducing hallucinations in visual question answering. Furthermore, this intersection opens new frontiers in accessibility, UI/UX evaluation, and collaborative robotics by allowing AI to anticipate human intent based on real-time visual attention.

4. Key insight
Eye tracking transforms human visual attention into a computable signal, allowing language models to see the world through human eyes. This gaze-grounding shifts AI from passive observation to active collaboration by aligning machine reasoning with human intent.

Paper Summaries

TransGaze-Object: Transformer Based Driver Gaze Object Prediction Framework in Real Driving

Authors: Pavan Kumar Sharma, Ayush Pande, Pranamesh Chakraborty
arXiv: 2609.10139v1

Summary

### 1. Research Question. How can we predict the specific traffic object a driver is looking at directly from face and scene data, rather than relying on intermediate Point-of-Gaze estimation? The paper aims to move beyond gaze zones and vectors to provide semantically meaningful object-level gaze information.

### 2. Positioning. Previous studies in driver gaze estimation typically represent gaze as a geometric vector, a 2D point, or a broad zone. This work fills the gap by introducing object-level gaze prediction. This approach offers a richer semantic understanding of driver attention in real driving scenarios.

### 3. Key Contribution. The main contribution is TransGaze-Object, an end-to-end Transformer based framework that directly predicts attended traffic objects from driver face and scene inputs. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset called UD-FSG. This dataset features synchronized face and scene images with object bounding boxes and gaze labels.

### 4. Methodology. The framework extracts facial features, specifically iris-weighted eye features, alongside spatial features of traffic objects. A Transformer cross-attention mechanism then computes similarity scores and attention weights between the driver gaze features and the traffic objects. These weights are used to predict the final gaze target.

### 5. Limitations. The overall prediction accuracy is \(60\%\), meaning a significant portion of gaze targets are still misclassified. The approach also relies on the availability of synchronized high-quality driver face and traffic scene data. This dependency might limit its applicability in poor lighting or adverse weather conditions.

### 6. Critical Evaluation. The direct prediction of gaze objects using a Transformer cross-attention mechanism is a strong conceptual shift from traditional Point-of-Gaze estimation. By bypassing the intermediate 2D point calculation, the model effectively reduces background confusion by a relative \(49.7\%\). However, the relatively modest \(60\%\) accuracy suggests that object-level gaze prediction in the wild remains a challenging problem for computer vision and gaze tracking models.

Do Cantonese-Adapted Language Models Better Predict Cantonese Reading? A Cross-Model Eye-Tracking Evaluation

Authors: Ziqi Zhang, Emmanuele Chersoni, Mohammad Momenian
arXiv: 2609.02163v2

Summary

1. Research Question
Does adapting language models specifically for the Cantonese language variety improve their ability to predict human reading patterns compared to general-purpose or Mandarin-oriented models?

2. Positioning
Information-theoretic measures from autoregressive language models are widely used to model human reading expectations, yet the psycholinguistic benefits of language-variety-specific training remain unclear. While recent NLP evaluations for Cantonese show mixed benefits from specialized training, this work bridges that gap by directly evaluating these models against naturalistic human eye-tracking data. It tests whether deeper linguistic adaptation translates to better cognitive alignment.

3. Key Contribution
The paper provides a cross-model eye-tracking evaluation comparing lightly and extensively adapted Cantonese language models against their base versions. It demonstrates that more extensive Cantonese-specific continued pretraining and instruction tuning generally yields a stronger predictive fit for human reading behavior. Furthermore, it reveals that model rankings fluctuate depending on the specific information-theoretic metric used, such as lexical surprisal versus entropy reduction.

4. Methodology
The authors use naturalistic Cantonese eye-tracking data to evaluate two pairs of models. These pairs are CKIP GPT-2 Tiny versus its lightly adapted JED351 derivative, and Qwen2.5-7B versus the extensively adapted CantoneseLLM-7B. From each model, they extract four information-theoretic measures including \( \text{lexical surprisal} \), \( \text{POS surprisal} \), \( \text{entropy before the target} \), and \( \text{entropy reduction} \) to assess psycholinguistic alignment.

5. Limitations
The study is limited by its focus on only two within-family adaptation contrasts, which may not capture the full landscape of Cantonese NLP models. Additionally, the divergent results across different information-theoretic metrics suggest that the evaluation framework itself might be highly sensitive to specific architectural or training nuances. The exclusive reliance on autoregressive language models also leaves out other architectures that might differently predict reading behavior.

6. Critical Evaluation
A major strength of this work is its direct linking of LLM adaptation strategies to human gaze data, providing valuable psycholinguistic grounding for language-specific fine-tuning. However, the conflicting metric outcomes, where entropy reduction favors a smaller and lightly adapted model over the larger extensively adapted one, highlight a weakness in our theoretical understanding of these measures. Overall, it is a solid empirical contribution that underscores the complexity of using LLMs as cognitive models for reading.

LandmarkLens: Predicting and Presenting Effective Landmarks for Mixed-Reality Urban Exploration

Authors: Chu Li, Yotam Sechayk, Jared Hwang et al.
arXiv: 2608.30142v1

Summary

### 1. Research Question. How do differences in sense of direction affect landmark attention during urban navigation? The paper also asks whether a mixed-reality system using a vision-language model can help poor navigators acquire spatial knowledge.

### 2. Positioning. Existing navigation tools prioritize route efficiency over spatial learning. This leaves users with poor spatial memory dependent on the system. This work bridges spatial cognition research and mixed-reality interfaces by using gaze behavior to inform AI-driven landmark highlighting.

### 3. Key Contribution. The paper introduces LandmarkLens. It is a mixed-reality navigation system that uses a vision-language model to automatically identify and highlight effective landmarks. The novelty lies in grounding the VLM landmark selection in empirical gaze data and verbal reports. This guided visual attention improves scene recognition for poor navigators.

### 4. Methodology. The researchers first conducted a VR study with 20 participants. They tracked eye gaze and verbal descriptions while participants navigated four Tokyo neighborhoods. They analyzed the gaze data to find systematic differences between good and poor sense of direction groups. They then used a vision-language model to recognize effective landmarks based on these findings and integrated it into a mixed-reality headset. Finally they evaluated the system with eight poor navigators.

### 5. Limitations. The evaluation study had a small sample size of only eight poor navigators. This limits the statistical power of the findings. The VR study was also limited to four Tokyo neighborhoods. This may restrict the generalizability of the gaze findings to other urban layouts. Furthermore the paper only tests scene recognition. This is a narrow metric for broader spatial learning.

### 6. Critical Evaluation. A major strength is the tight integration of psychophysical data and modern generative AI to solve a real-world accessibility issue. The approach is elegant because it uses human gaze as the ground truth for what makes a landmark effective. However the reliance on VLMs for real-time highlighting introduces potential latency and robustness issues in dynamic real-world lighting. These system performance metrics are not fully explored.

Predicting Radiologist Expertise from 3D Gaze Patterns During CT Interpretation

Authors: Leila Khaertdinova, Anna Anikina, Claudia Mello-Thoms et al.
arXiv: 2608.23836v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Can 3D eye-tracking patterns of radiologists during CT interpretation be used to accurately classify their level of expertise? The paper aims to solve the problem of objective, process-based expertise assessment in volumetric medical imaging.

2. Positioning
Prior research on eye-tracking in medical imaging has primarily focused on 2D images. This work fills the gap in extending gaze-based expertise assessment to volumetric CT scans, where spatial navigation is significantly more complex. It bridges the divide between visual search behavior analysis and modern transformer architectures in 3D medical contexts.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is a novel gaze-informed transformer framework that classifies radiologist expertise using 3D CT reading sessions. It introduces two specific integration techniques for combining gaze data with a DINOv2 vision backbone. These techniques are a learnable log-space bias in self-attention and gaze-weighted pooling of patch embeddings.

4. Methodology
The authors use a DINOv2 backbone to process volumetric CT data and integrate radiologist fixation patterns. Gaze data is incorporated into the model through a learnable log-space bias within the self-attention mechanism and by weighting patch embeddings during pooling. The framework was trained and evaluated on 182 reading sessions from five radiologists with varying experience levels.

5. Limitations
The study is limited by a relatively small sample size of only five radiologists and 182 reading sessions. This could restrict the generalizability of the model to other institutions or different radiology sub-specialties. The abstract also does not detail the diversity of pathologies in the CT dataset, which might impact the robustness of the expertise assessment.

6. Critical Evaluation
The work is a strong technical achievement, achieving an ROC-AUC of \(0.91\) and an F1 score of \(0.86\) by successfully fusing 3D gaze data with vision transformers. The explicit mathematical integration of gaze into attention biases is a particularly elegant solution for multimodal AI. However, the practical deployment of such a system depends heavily on the availability and calibration of eye-tracking hardware in clinical reading rooms.

Matched Outcomes, Divergent Gaze: How Foveated MLLMs Search Compared to Humans

Authors: Mohamed Amine Kerkouri, Marouane Tliba, Aladine Chetouani et al.
arXiv: 2608.16514v1

Summary

1. Research Question
Do multimodal large language models (MLLMs) perform visual search like humans when given identical foveated input? The paper investigates whether these models can replicate human eye movement scanpaths during goal-directed visual search.

2. Positioning
This work positions itself at the intersection of cognitive vision research and multimodal AI evaluation. It highlights a critical gap in existing literature where standard answer alignment and saliency metrics fail to capture the temporal dynamics of visual search. The paper argues that matching retinal input is insufficient to claim human-like vision if the underlying gaze process remains fundamentally different.

3. Key Contribution
The main contribution is the demonstration that MLLMs and humans dissociate sharply on process metrics despite matching on outcome metrics. The authors show that while MLLMs detect targets efficiently, their scanpaths are low-entropy, large-amplitude, and highly self-consistent. This reveals that current zero-shot MLLMs use a non-serial architecture, a limitation that standard spatial and outcome metrics completely miss.

4. Methodology
The authors evaluate three general-purpose MLLMs on the COCO-Search18 dataset by driving them fixation by fixation through human-matched foveated views. They assess model performance across three distinct axes. These are target presence decisions, target acquisition efficiency, and the gaze process itself. This setup forces the models to process visual information sequentially just like human foveal vision.

5. Limitations
The study is constrained by its evaluation of only three general-purpose MLLMs on a single dataset. The authors also acknowledge that no degradation regime successfully recovers human-like search at human-like success rates. This suggests a fundamental architectural barrier in current models rather than a simple input limitation.

6. Critical Evaluation
This work is highly valuable for the gaze-tracking and AI communities because it rigorously proves that output accuracy does not equal human-like processing. Its strict separation of outcome versus process metrics makes the findings highly actionable for future benchmark design. However, a weakness is that it primarily diagnoses the problem without proposing concrete architectural modifications to make MLLMs more human-like in their temporal dynamics.
